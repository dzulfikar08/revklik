Cookieless Analytics: Why It Is the Future of Web Tracking

May 12, 2026 5 min read By the RevKlik Team

Third-party cookies are officially dead. Google Chrome, the browser used by 65% of internet users, has removed support for them. Safari and Firefox did so years earlier. For businesses that rely on analytics to make decisions, this is a fundamental change.

But the good news: cookieless analytics is not only possible, it is often more accurate than the old methods.

What Is Cookieless Analytics?

Cookieless analytics is a way of tracking visitors that does not store cookie files in the user's browser. Instead of relying on cookies to identify visitors, the system uses a combination of other techniques that respect privacy more.

The main difference is simple: traditional analytics leaves a small file (a cookie) in the visitor's browser to "remember" who they are. Cookieless analytics leaves nothing behind. All processing happens on the server side.

Why Are Third-Party Cookies Dying?

Three big pressures are killing cookies:

  1. Privacy regulation: GDPR in Europe, PDP in Indonesia, and similar regulations around the world make using cookies without explicit consent illegal.
  2. Modern browsers: Safari, Firefox, and finally Chrome block third-party cookies by default.
  3. The AI era: Users are increasingly uncomfortable with aggressive cross-site tracking, and AI technology makes tracking feel more invasive than ever.
According to a 2025 eMarketer study, 72% of internet users actively reject cookie tracking when given the choice. This is not a small trend. It is a mass shift in behavior.

How Does Cookieless Analytics Work?

Three main methods used by modern cookieless analytics:

1. Session-Based Tracking

Each time a visitor opens your site, the system creates a unique session ID on the server (not in the browser). The session lasts while the visitor is active. When the browser is closed, the session ends and no trace is left on the user's device.

This is enough to track the user journey within a single visit: which pages were viewed, how long they stayed on each page, and where they left the site.

2. Light Fingerprinting

Some analytics tools use a fingerprint, a combination of browser characteristics such as screen size, language, and time zone, to recognize the same visitor without cookies. This method is controversial when done aggressively, but used minimally and anonymously, it can help connect repeat visits without violating privacy.

Important

Invasive fingerprinting (using the list of installed fonts, browser plugins, and so on) is considered a privacy violation in some jurisdictions. Use only basic characteristics that do not identify individuals.

3. First-Party Server-Side Processing

Instead of sending data to third-party servers (which requires cross-domain cookies), all data is processed on your own server or on an edge server you control. This means no data is shared with ad networks, and there is no cross-site tracking.

What Do You Lose Without Cookies?

To be honest, there is a trade-off. Without cookies, you cannot:

  • Track the same visitor across devices (for example from phone to laptop)
  • Recognize a visitor who returns weeks or months later
  • Automatically connect analytics data with ad platforms for retargeting

But for most businesses, especially SaaS and digital products, these three things are not as important as you think. What matters most is where visitors come from, what they do on your site, and whether they convert. All of this can be tracked without cookies.

When Do You Still Need Cookies?

There is one scenario where first-party cookies are still useful: when visitors log in to an account on your site. In that case you already have the user's identity through your authentication system, and the session cookie only keeps them logged in. This is legal and unrelated to the third-party cookie problem.

For analytics, you can link session data to user accounts without a separate tracking cookie.

Conclusion

Cookieless analytics is not a compromise, it is an upgrade. You get enough data to make business decisions, without violating visitor privacy, without slowing down your site, and without regulatory risk.

Cookies are dead. Good analytics is not.
